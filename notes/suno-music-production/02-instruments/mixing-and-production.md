## Core Concept

**EQ is about ownership, not decoration.** Every instrument in a rock mix competes for the same handful of frequency bands; a "good mix" is really a set of decisions about which instrument *owns* each band and which ones get carved out of it. The three recurring ownership fights in a rock mix:

- **Sub/low-end split**: kick drum owns roughly **60-100 Hz** (punch and weight), bass guitar owns roughly **80-250 Hz** (the sustained pitched low end). The classic rule of thumb is "kick owns 80 Hz, bass owns 100-120 Hz" — i.e. each instrument's fundamental peak is nudged to a slightly different sub-band so they reinforce rather than mask each other. Content below **25-45 Hz** is usually cut outright (rumble, not audible low end, just eats headroom).
- **The mud zone (200-500 Hz)**: guitars, bass, kick, and vocals all pile up energy here simultaneously — this is the single most common reason a rock mix sounds "muddy" or "boxy" rather than clear. Standard fix is a broad cut in this range on the instruments that don't need it there (commonly the kick and rhythm guitars), leaving room for whichever part is actually supposed to carry that band.
- **Guitar vs. vocal presence conflict**: vocal clarity/intelligibility lives at roughly **1-5 kHz**, with a narrower "forward, in-your-face" presence boost around **2-5 kHz**. Rhythm guitars often carry midrange energy in the same **1-3 kHz** window for their own clarity — when both fight for that band, the vocal usually wins the pocket (guitars get a modest cut there) since a rock mix without an intelligible lead vocal reads as unfinished in a way that a slightly less crisp guitar doesn't.

**Compression controls dynamic range, not "punch" as a vague adjective.** A compressor turns down anything louder than its **threshold**, by a factor set by the **ratio** (4:1 means a signal 4 dB over threshold comes out only 1 dB over), engaging after a delay set by **attack** (how fast it clamps down once threshold is crossed) and releasing after a delay set by **release** (how fast it lets go once the signal drops back below threshold). Fast attack tames transients (the "crack" of a snare, the consonant hit of a vocal) at the cost of aggression; slow attack lets the transient through and only compresses the sustain, which reads as more natural. Compression is the right tool for taming a performance's dynamic range so it sits consistently in a mix (a vocal that's whispered in verses and belted in choruses needs it); it is the wrong tool for fixing a fundamentally bad performance or arrangement — no compressor setting fixes a chorus that isn't written to feel bigger than the verse (see [../03-songwriting/dynamics-and-arrangement.md](../03-songwriting/dynamics-and-arrangement.md)).

**Reverb and delay both add a sense of space, but by different mechanisms.** Reverb simulates thousands of overlapping reflections off a space's surfaces, decaying continuously over time. Delay repeats the source as discrete, separated echoes. Both are described by **decay time** (reverb) or **delay time** (echo spacing), and both can be used subtly (a sense of "the room the performance happened in") or as an obvious effect in its own right (a huge gated snare, a rhythmic slapback).

**Gain staging is level management through the whole signal chain, not just the final fader.** Every stage from the original recording/generation through each processing step should sit at a healthy level — loud enough to clear the noise floor, quiet enough to leave headroom (commonly targeted at peaks around **-6 dBFS** during mixing, well below the **0 dBFS** point where digital clipping flattens the waveform and adds harsh distortion). Plugins modeled on analog hardware are often level-sensitive: overdriving the input into a plugin causes it to saturate or distort in a way that turning the output down afterward cannot undo. This matters even when there's no literal mixing console in the loop (see Suno Translation below) — it's the conceptual reason "loud" and "clean" pull in opposite directions and why an already-loud source track leaves less room to sculpt it further.

**Stereo imaging is a separate axis from EQ/dynamics — it's about *where* something sits between the L and R speakers/headphone channels, not its tone or level over time.** Two channels only: a mix is "balanced" when neither channel is systematically louder across the whole song (an imbalance reads as the whole mix leaning toward one ear), and "wide" when there's meaningful difference *between* the channels' content (mid/side energy) rather than both channels carrying near-identical signal (which collapses toward mono-sounding, even in a stereo file). These are independent problems — a mix can be perfectly balanced L=R in overall level while still sounding narrow, or slightly imbalanced while still sounding wide.

**Dolby Atmos and other object-based spatial audio are a different technology tier, not an extension of stereo panning.** Traditional stereo (and 5.1/7.1 surround) are *channel-based* — the mix is baked into a fixed number of speaker feeds. Atmos is *object-based* — each sound is stored with 3D position metadata (left/right, front/back, **and height**) and a compatible playback device renders it live based on however many speakers (or a simulated equivalent for headphones) are actually present. This means an Atmos mix isn't something you get by processing a stereo file more cleverly — it requires the source to have been produced (or later up-mixed with dedicated tools) with per-object spatial data from the start, which a 2-channel stereo export fundamentally does not contain.

## Practical Application

- **Vocal EQ pass**: cut the 200-500 Hz mud range if the vocal sounds boxy, boost narrowly around 2-5 kHz for presence/forward clarity, and high-pass below roughly 80-100 Hz (a lead vocal has no useful content down there, and leaving it in just eats headroom other instruments need).
- **Kick/bass low-end split**: if the low end sounds like one indistinct rumble rather than two locked parts, that's an ownership problem, not a "make it louder" problem — narrow each instrument's peak into its own sub-band (kick ~60-100 Hz, bass ~80-250 Hz) rather than boosting both across the same range. See [bass.md](bass.md) for the kick-lock rhythmic relationship this EQ split is reinforcing.
- **Rock vocal compression**: typical starting point is a **4:1 to 6:1 ratio**, medium-fast attack (~10-15 ms) to let the initial consonant through before clamping, medium release (~80-100 ms), targeting **4-6 dB of gain reduction** on the loudest phrases. A hard-knee setting (compression engages abruptly right at threshold, rather than gradually) suits the more aggressive character rock vocals usually want.
- **Bus/glue compression**: much gentler — **2:1 ratio**, soft knee, slower attack (~20-50 ms, so it doesn't choke transients) and slower release (~100-300 ms, to avoid audible "pumping"), targeting only **2-4 dB of gain reduction**. The goal is cohesion (making a group of tracks feel like one thing), not obvious dynamic squashing.
- **Reverb choice by section**: a short room or plate (low decay, under ~1.5 s) keeps a verse vocal intimate and present; a longer hall (decay several seconds) opens up a bridge or outro for a "huge space" feeling; gated reverb (a normal-length reverb tail abruptly cut off by a noise gate rather than fading naturally) is the classic 80s drum/vocal effect — big-sounding attack with none of the wash of a full tail. See [../03-songwriting/dynamics-and-arrangement.md](../03-songwriting/dynamics-and-arrangement.md) for pairing reverb size with the quiet/huge arc of a song.
- **Delay choice**: slapback (short delay, roughly 80-150 ms, minimal or no feedback) doubles and thickens a vocal or rhythm guitar without reading as an obvious "echo" effect — a rockabilly/classic-rock staple. Ping-pong delay (repeats alternating left/right across the stereo field) adds motion and width, typically reserved for a lead guitar solo or a specific ear-candy moment rather than the whole mix, since constant stereo-bouncing gets fatiguing.
- **Gain-staging discipline even without a mixing console**: keep every processing step's output comfortably below clipping, and don't chase "loud" early in the chain — loudness is the last thing added (via a limiter/maximizer), not something baked in at every stage. Chasing loudness early leaves nothing for a final master to add without further squashing dynamics.
- **Measuring L/R balance objectively (don't trust headphones alone)**: perceived "leaning left/right" can come from the headphones themselves, ear fatigue, or an actual imbalance in the file — the only way to tell them apart is to measure the file. Load both channels (e.g. Python `soundfile` + `numpy`), compute **RMS** (root-mean-square, the average energy) of each channel separately, convert to dB (`20*log10(rms)`), and compare `dB_L - dB_R`. Practical thresholds: **under ±0.5 dB is inaudible** (treat as balanced), **±0.5 to ±1.5 dB is borderline** (a trained ear might notice, most listeners won't), **beyond ±1.5 dB is an audible lean**. This only needs stdlib-adjacent tools (`numpy`, `soundfile`) — no DAW required, so it's cheap to check every generated take as a matter of course rather than trusting a subjective "sounds off" impression.
- **Measuring stereo width**: convert L/R to **mid** (`(L+R)/2`, the mono-compatible center content) and **side** (`(L-R)/2`, the content that differs between channels — this is literally what carries "width"), then compare their RMS as a ratio (`side/mid`). A ratio near 0 means the mix is nearly mono (both channels almost identical); higher ratios mean more genuine stereo spread. There's no universal "correct" number — it's genre- and arrangement-dependent — but it's a useful single-number way to A/B two takes of the same song for width instead of relying on ear alone.
- **Vocal-vs-arrangement masking risk is highest exactly where dynamics are working as intended.** The "vocal owns the 1-5 kHz presence pocket" ownership rule above is usually framed against a rock guitar, but the same ownership fight happens against *any* instrument with midrange energy — piano and string swells included, not just guitar. The riskiest moments are precisely the ones a good dynamic arc calls for (see [../03-songwriting/dynamics-and-arrangement.md](../03-songwriting/dynamics-and-arrangement.md)): a build-up or a "strings swell in" cue landing right as a vocal is sustaining a note is the moment arrangement density peaks *while* the vocal is also trying to peak — without an explicit ownership instruction, the two compete for the same headroom at the same time, so a swell can bury the very vocal line it's supposed to be lifting. Don't assume "dynamic swell" and "clear vocal" are automatically compatible instructions — they're in tension exactly at the swell moment, and that tension needs to be resolved explicitly (see Suno Translation below), not left implicit.

## Suno Translation

Suno exposes none of the above as knobs — no EQ, no compressor, no reverb send, no fader. All of it has to be requested as **text descriptors in the Style field**, which act as proxies the model has learned to associate with specific production choices from its training data (see [../04-suno/style-prompt-formula.md](../04-suno/style-prompt-formula.md) for how these tags compete for budget against genre/instrument/mood tags). Suno also reportedly has no separate mastering stage — what generates is the finished audio, so "mastering" tags are really requests for the generation itself to already sound loud/polished/raw, not a post-process applied afterward.

| Mix/master concept | Suno Style tag(s) that approximate it |
|---|---|
| Broadcast-clean, well-balanced mix | `polished production`, `radio-ready mix`, `crisp mix`, `professional mastering`, `modern master` |
| Deliberately unpolished/aggressive mix | `raw mix`, `dry and punchy`, `unpolished production` |
| Lo-fi/degraded aesthetic (intentional, not a defect) | `lo-fi`, `tape hiss`, `vinyl crackle`, `bedroom recording`, `warm analog` |
| Heavy compression / commercial loudness | `compressed and loud`, `loud mastered` |
| Vocal-forward mix / vocal not buried under a swell or dense arrangement | `vocal-forward mix`, `intimate close-mic vocals mixed forward and clear`, `vocals sit forward even during swells` — reinforce with an inline cue (see [../04-suno/metatags-and-lyric-markup.md](../04-suno/metatags-and-lyric-markup.md)) placed directly on the swell/build line itself, e.g. `(strings swell in, vocal stays forward)`, so the instruction lands exactly where the masking risk peaks rather than only as a general Style-field statement covering the whole song |
| Wide dynamic range, quiet/loud contrast preserved | `dynamic`, `quiet verse to loud chorus dynamic` |
| Reverb-heavy, spacious | `reverb-drenched`, `spacious and wide`, `stadium reverb` |
| Gated reverb specifically (80s drum/vocal snap) | `gated reverb`, `gated plate reverb` — see [../03-songwriting/genre-conventions.md](../03-songwriting/genre-conventions.md) for the 80s-arena-rock profile this pairs with |
| Tight, dry, minimal-space mix | `dry and punchy`, `close and intimate` |
| Analog warmth / saturation as a texture | `tape saturation`, `warm analog` |

Judging output: since there's no way to isolate and fix "the bass is 3 dB too loud" the way a real mixing session would, the practical workflow is to describe the *target character* as precisely as the tags above allow, generate, and treat a wrong result as a signal to adjust wording (e.g. `polished production` → `radio-ready mix` if the first didn't move the needle enough) rather than a mix problem to fix in a nonexistent console. See [../04-suno/iteration-strategy.md](../04-suno/iteration-strategy.md) for the one-variable-per-generation discipline this implies.

**Stereo imaging and spatial audio have no Suno controls at all — not even the indirect Style-tag-as-proxy mechanism that covers EQ/compression/reverb above.** There is no pan control, no stereo-width tag, no "put the strings on the left" mechanism, and Style-field phrases like `wide stereo` or `spacious stereo image` have not been observed to reliably move the needle (they likely get absorbed into the same bucket as `spacious and wide` reverb-character tags rather than doing anything to the L/R balance specifically). Any real fix for a leaning or narrow-sounding stereo file has to happen in **post-processing on the exported audio** (see Practical Application measurement technique above, then correct with a simple per-channel gain adjustment). **Dolby Atmos is entirely out of reach starting from Suno**: Suno exports plain stereo (2-channel) audio with no object/height metadata, so there is nothing to "turn into" Atmos without a full manual up-mix in dedicated software (e.g. a DAW with a Dolby Atmos Renderer plugin) — a separate production pipeline, not a Suno prompting concern.
